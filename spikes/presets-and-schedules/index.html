Presets and schedules — behaviour lab

Thesis: a preset is a partial overlay on the panel, and the panel is a stack of layers, last wins: project → your baseline → active presets (in priority order) → your changes. An activation holds a preset on, from one source, for a while; a schedule is only a trigger that makes activations. So every control can say why it has its value, and turning a preset off shows exactly what was under it. This page runs the model in lab/presets/model (Rust, 33 statements) rule for rule, on a house's eave lights. Move the clock, cut the power, press presets, edit schedules, and see if it behaves.

1The panel

What you'd see in Play mode on your phone. Active presets are the chips; each control's left bar and label say which layer its value comes from, in that layer's colour. Turning a control writes your changes (the top layer), which end by themselves at the schedule's next change. Tap a chip to edit that preset in place; tap Baseline to edit the panel defaults.

2The schedule, three ways

Each bar is one activation a schedule will make (or made), in its preset's colour; hatched bars were pressed by hand; dashed outlines were turned off. The dark strip under each week is what the lights do, computed by the model every 15 minutes from the schedules alone (the "pretend it's…" copy): its colour is the preset that owns the pattern, its strength the brightness, black is off. Days run noon to noon by default so an evening is never cut at midnight (switch it in the strip above). Click anywhere on a calendar to pretend it's that moment — the panel shows that time without moving the board's clock.

4What building it turned up

Found while writing the toy's statements and this page. Each has a button that sets the scene.

My lean.
  1. Calendar: 2A (weeks stacked) for the schedule's own page, because lights live in the evening and a week row shows a whole evening unbroken, the "what the lights do" strip reads at a glance, and lanes by source make two Halloween schedules visibly two things. 2C is familiar but squeezes evenings into narrow columns and hides which source made what; 2B is the best for a week of detail and the worst for October.
  2. The model holds up. Every journey in the requirements is a statement that passes (R1–R8). The two that felt wrong to build are F1 (your change ending minutes later) and F2 (switching a schedule off leaves tonight's on): both are rules we chose, not bugs.
  3. F1 is the one to decide before planning. My lean: your changes end per control, when the next change touches that control — brightness you turned survives Halloween starting, and gives way to Late dim.
